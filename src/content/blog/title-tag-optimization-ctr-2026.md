For years, the standard title tag advice was simple: put the keyword near the front, keep it under 60 characters, done. That advice still produces technically correct titles. It stopped producing titles that win clicks somewhere around the point every competitor started following the exact same formula, and a SERP full of identical keyword-first titles gives a searcher no reason to pick one result over another.

## Specificity beats keyword placement

A title that says "Best CRM Software 2026" competes against fifteen other results saying almost the same thing. A title that says "CRM Software Compared: Pricing for Teams Under 20 People" gives the searcher a concrete reason to believe this particular result answers their specific situation. The keyword is still there, doing its job for relevance matching, but the words around it are what earn the click once the page is sitting next to nine others that technically match the query just as well.

Numbers work the same way when they're real. "How much does a kitchen renovation cost in Dubai" turns into a stronger title with an actual figure or range attached, "Kitchen Renovation Costs in Dubai: AED 25,000 to 90,000 Explained," because it answers the implicit question in the search before the click even happens. A vague number or a round figure that reads as a guess undoes the effect; searchers can tell the difference between a real data point and a placeholder.

## Matching intent, not just the query string

Google's ranking systems have gotten better at distinguishing a page that mentions a keyword from a page that satisfies what the searcher wants, and titles need to reflect that same distinction. A comparison query deserves a title that signals comparison. A how-to query deserves a title that signals a process, ideally with a hint at how long or involved that process is. Writing a generic, keyword-matched title for a page that's built to satisfy a specific intent undersells what the page delivers, and searchers who scan titles quickly will skip past it for a competitor's title that states the value more directly.

## What changes between a weak title and a strong one

- Weak: "SEO Services Dubai | Digital Marketing Agency" versus strong: "SEO Services in Dubai: Pricing and Process for B2B Companies"
- Weak: "Best Running Shoes 2026" versus strong: "12 Running Shoes Tested for Overpronation, Ranked by Price"
- Weak: "How to Learn Python" versus strong: "Learn Python in 90 Days: A Realistic Study Plan for Beginners"

Each strong version keeps the core keyword intact while adding a specific, credible detail that a generic version can't offer.

## Length and Google's rewriting behaviour

Google typically displays somewhere around 50 to 60 characters of a title before truncating, though the real cutoff depends on pixel width rather than character count, so wider characters truncate sooner. Beyond length, Google rewrites titles it judges as a poor match for the query or for the page's actual content, which happens more often with titles stuffed with keywords, brand names repeated unnecessarily, or generic phrasing that doesn't reflect what's genuinely on the page. A title that's honest about page content and reasonably concise gets left alone far more often than one written primarily to game rankings.

## Testing titles with Search Console CTR data

The only reliable way to know whether a title change works is to test it against real performance, not intuition. Pull the Performance report for the specific page, note the baseline CTR and average position over a stable period, change the title once, and wait long enough (typically three to four weeks) to separate a genuine shift from normal ranking noise. Testing multiple title changes on the same page too close together makes it impossible to attribute a CTR shift to any single change, which defeats the purpose of testing at all.

The pages worth testing first are the ones ranking in positions three through eight with CTR meaningfully below what that position typically earns; that gap is usually the clearest sign the title, not the ranking, is what's costing the clicks.
