Two competitors ranking on page one for the same commercial keyword tells you less than it seems to. It tells you the term is contested. It says nothing about the twenty adjacent questions their audience is asking that neither of them has bothered answering yet, and that's where content gap analysis earns its keep.

## Why matching keywords is the slow way in

Targeting the same head terms an established competitor already ranks for means competing against their existing backlink profile, their domain history, and whatever authority they've built on that exact topic for years. A newer or smaller site chasing the identical keyword is playing a game weighted against it from the start. Content gap analysis flips the approach: instead of asking what competitors are ranking for, it asks what their audience is searching for that they haven't written about at all.

## Running the audit properly

Start with a straight content audit of two or three real competitors, cataloguing every piece they've published on the topic and grouping it by subtopic rather than by individual keyword. Patterns show up fast. A competitor with fifteen articles on pricing and none on implementation timelines, integration limitations, or what happens after year one of a contract has an obvious blind spot, and it usually exists because writing about implementation friction is less flattering than writing about pricing.

From there, a keyword gap tool such as Ahrefs' Content Gap or Semrush's Keyword Gap pulls the terms competitors rank for that a given site doesn't, which is useful but incomplete on its own. These tools only surface keywords someone is already ranking for. They can't show a real question nobody has written a good answer to yet, which is often the higher-value find.

That's where audience research earns its place in the process rather than being an optional add-on. Reddit threads, review sites, and a company's own support tickets surface the actual language customers use when they're confused, frustrated, or comparing options, and a surprising amount of that language never shows up in any keyword tool because search volume data lags behind a genuinely new question. A support ticket log with the same unresolved question appearing forty times in three months is a stronger content signal than a keyword tool showing 200 monthly searches for a related term, because it's proof of unmet demand from people already in the buying process, not a proxy for it.

## Prioritising what to write first

Not every gap is worth filling immediately. A genuine gap with low competition and clear buying intent behind it, someone asking a specific implementation question right before a purchase decision, should get written before a broader, more competitive topic that would take months to rank for even without direct competition. Weighing gaps by realistic opportunity against the effort a piece would take, rather than by search volume alone, keeps a content calendar from filling up with easy-to-write pieces that don't move anything commercially.

A gap worth chasing usually has three things lined up at once: real, demonstrated demand from a source outside keyword tools, low or no direct competition for that specific angle, and a plausible link to a buying decision rather than pure curiosity. Missing any one of the three doesn't disqualify a topic, but it changes how much priority it deserves relative to everything else competing for the same production slot.

## Feeding it into the calendar

A content gap analysis that sits in a spreadsheet and never reaches the editorial calendar was time spent for nothing. The output of this process should become the next quarter's content calendar directly, ranked by the opportunity-versus-effort scoring rather than by whoever pitches loudest in a planning meeting, and revisited every few months, because competitors close their own gaps and new ones open as products, pricing, and customer questions change.
