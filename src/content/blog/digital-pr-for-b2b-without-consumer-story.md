Consumer brands have an easy digital PR lever: a viral product angle, a seasonal hook, a story that works because everyone is a potential customer. B2B companies selling enterprise software or industrial equipment don't have that option, and pitching a mass-market angle for a niche product to a journalist who covers general news gets ignored correctly, because there genuinely isn't a story there for that audience.

## The real problem isn't a lack of stories

B2B companies often assume digital PR isn't available to them because their product doesn't have a consumer hook. The actual issue is narrower: they're pitching the wrong audience with the wrong kind of story. A logistics software company has nothing interesting to say to a lifestyle publication. It can have plenty to say to a supply chain trade outlet, provided the pitch offers something that publication's readers and editors can't get anywhere else: a specific number, a genuine opinion, or a pattern only that company can see.

## Original research and survey data

A tightly scoped survey of a defined professional audience, a few hundred logistics managers, or procurement leads at mid-size manufacturers, produces something trade journalists want: a citable statistic specific to their beat. This works better at a narrow scope than a broad one. A survey of "500 business leaders" about generic workplace trends competes with dozens of similar studies published every month. A survey of "200 warehouse operations managers on how they're handling the current shipping delays" has almost no competition, because almost nobody else has bothered to ask that specific audience that specific question.

## Executive commentary and thought leadership

Trade press runs on expert quotes and opinion pieces, and B2B executives are often better positioned to provide those than they realise. The pitch that works isn't "our CEO is available to comment on industry trends," which every company sends and most journalists ignore. It's a specific, sometimes contrarian position on something happening in the industry right now: a prediction that goes against consensus, a criticism of a common practice, or a firsthand account of a change the executive has already lived through inside the business. Vague availability doesn't get placed. A real opinion does.

## Data already sitting inside the business

Every B2B company generates data nobody outside it has ever seen: transaction patterns, usage trends across a customer base, seasonal shifts in demand, response times, adoption curves. Pulled out, anonymised, and framed around a question the industry genuinely cares about, this proprietary data is often the strongest original story available, and it costs nothing extra to produce since the company already has it. A payments company sitting on transaction volume data has a story about spending trends most publications would want. A recruitment platform sitting on application data has a story about hiring trends. Most of this data never leaves internal dashboards.

## Examples that earned real coverage

A B2B accounting software company analysing its own customer invoicing data to publish "average payment delay by industry" earned coverage across finance and small-business trade press, because it answered a question those publications' readers genuinely have. A cybersecurity firm publishing a survey of IT managers on password policy compliance got picked up by security trade outlets specifically because the finding was mildly uncomfortable rather than a company promoting its own product. Neither story needed a consumer angle. Both needed a specific, verifiable finding.

## Realistic expectations on volume and quality

B2B digital PR earns fewer total links than a well-executed consumer campaign, and that comparison is the wrong one to make in the first place. A consumer campaign chasing viral pickup might land two hundred links from a mix of relevant and irrelevant sites. A B2B campaign targeting the right trade press might land fifteen to thirty links, almost all of them from publications the company's actual buyers read, with domain authority and topical relevance that outweighs the raw count. Judging a B2B campaign by consumer PR volume benchmarks sets it up to look like a failure when it's working exactly as it should.
